Topic 3 — What "Dimensions" Mean in Data

In AI, dimension = the number of values used to describe one thing.

Concrete example — a user profile
Say you describe each user with 4 numbers:
[age, income, session_length, purchase_count]
[28,  75000,  12.5,           3             ]
This user lives in 4-dimensional space. You can't visualize 4D — that's fine. The math works regardless.

Why dimensionality matters in AI
Higher dimensions = richer description
A word embedding in a real LLM isn't 4-dimensional. It's 768, 1536, or 3072 dimensions.
The word "bank" might be represented as a 768-dimension vector. Those 768 numbers capture:

Is it related to finance?
Is it related to rivers?
Is it a noun or verb?
What words appear near it?

More dimensions = more nuance the model can store. (meaning of nuance the smaller difference between two things)

Your data matrix has two dimension counts — don't mix them up
Training dataset: 10,000 rows × 768 columns

10,000 = number of samples (users, sentences, images, inputs)
768    = number of features per sample (the dimensionality)

The curse of dimensionality — one thing to know
As dimensions grow, data gets sparse.
Simple example — if you have users on a 1D line (just age), two users aged 25 and 27 are close together. Add 767 more dimensions and suddenly everyone is far apart from everyone else.
This is why in AI you sometimes reduce dimensions — compressing 768D down to 2D just for visualization, or compressing 10,000D down to 256D to remove noise.
You'll see this come up again in embeddings and vector search.

The three ways "dimensions" gets used in AI conversations
    When someone says                                       They mean
"This embedding is 768-dimensional"         Each item is described by 768 numbers
"The input dimension is 512"                The model expects 512 numbers as input
"Reduce the dimensionality"                 Compress to fewer numbers while keeping meaning

Topic 4 — Dot Product

What it actually measures — this is the insight
The dot product measures how much two vectors point in the same direction.
Result                      Meaning
Large positive      Vectors point same direction — very similar
Near zero           Vectors are unrelated — perpendicular
Large negative      Vectors point opposite directions — very different

The connection to LLMs — preview
When you type a query and the LLM searches its knowledge:
Your query    → converted to a vector
Every concept the model knows → also vectors

Dot product of query with each concept
→ highest scores = most relevant concepts
→ model focuses attention there

Topic 5 - Probabilities Basics 

it's just basics of all terms used in probaility 

probaility - 
P(event) = value between 0 and 1

0   = impossible
1   = certain
0.7 = happens 70% of the time


Distribution - 
A distribution is when you assign probabilities to every possible outcome, and they all add up to 1.
Simple example — next word prediction after "I drink coffee every":
"morning"  → 0.50
"day"      → 0.25
"night"    → 0.15
"hour"     → 0.08
"week"     → 0.02
            ------
Total       = 1.00  ← always sums to 1

Mean - 
Mean — the average, but think of it as "center of gravity"
Scores = [60, 70, 80, 90, 100]

Mean = (60 + 70 + 80 + 90 + 100) / 5 = 80

Variance - 
Variance — how spread out the values are
Group A scores: [78, 79, 80, 81, 82]  → mean=80, low variance
Group B scores: [20, 50, 80, 95, 99]  → mean=80, high variance
Same mean, completely different distributions.
In AI this matters because:
Low variance model  → gives similar confident answers every time
High variance model → unpredictable, sometimes brilliant, sometimes wrong


Conditional probability-
Conditional probability — the core of language modeling
P(B | A) means "probability of B given A already happened"
LLMs are entirely built on this:
P("morning" | "I drink coffee every")  = 0.50
P("day"     | "I drink coffee every")  = 0.25
Every token is predicted using all previous tokens as context. 
The entire transformer architecture is a machine for computing this conditional probability efficiently.

Topic 7 -  SoftMax

The problem it solves

After a neural network does its matrix multiplications, you get raw scores called logits. These are just arbitrary numbers — can be negative, can be huge, don't sum to anything meaningful.

Raw logits after processing "The capital of France is":

"Paris"   →  8.2
"London"  →  2.1
"Berlin"  →  1.4
"Lyon"    →  0.9

These aren't probabilities yet. 8.2 doesn't mean 82%. You need them to:

All be between 0 and 1
Sum to exactly 1

That's what softmax does.

What softmax does — step by step

Step 1 — Exponentiate every number (raise e to the power of each)

e^8.2  =  3640.9
e^2.1  =    8.2
e^1.4  =    4.1
e^0.9  =    2.5

Sum    =  3655.7

Step 2 — Divide each by the sum

Paris   →  3640.9 / 3655.7  =  0.996
London  →     8.2 / 3655.7  =  0.002
Berlin  →     4.1 / 3655.7  =  0.001
Lyon    →     2.5 / 3655.7  =  0.001
                              -------
                              1.000  ✅

Now you have a valid probability distribution.

The key behavior of softmax — amplification

Notice what happened. The raw scores weren't that far apart:

Paris  8.2  vs  London  2.1  → ratio of ~4x

After softmax:

Paris  0.996  vs  London  0.002  → ratio of ~500x

Softmax amplifies the winner and crushes the losers.

This is intentional. Small differences in raw scores become large differences in probability. The model becomes decisive.

Why exponentiation specifically?

Two reasons:

1 — e^x is always positive
    No matter how negative the logit, probability stays above 0
    Every word always has some tiny chance

2 — e^x grows fast
    Bigger logit → exponentially bigger probability
    Creates that amplification effect

This connects to the next topic — log and exponential functions — which is why they appear everywhere in ML.

topic 8  -Log and Exponential Functions

Exponential — e^x

e is just a number — approximately 2.718

e^0  =  1
e^1  =  2.718
e^2  =  7.389
e^3  = 20.086
e^10 = 22026

Key behavior — grows explosively, always stays positive.

No matter how negative your input:

e^-100  =  a very tiny number, but never zero or negative

This is why softmax uses it — guarantees all probabilities stay positive.

Logarithm — the inverse of exponential

If e^x asks "what do I get if I raise e to power x?"

Log asks the reverse — "what power do I need to raise e to, in order to get x?"

e^3  = 20.086  →  log(20.086) = 3
e^2  =  7.389  →  log(7.389)  = 2
e^1  =  2.718  →  log(2.718)  = 1
e^0  =  1      →  log(1)      = 0

They undo each other — like Math.sqrt() and Math.pow(x, 2) in Java.

Key behavior of log — shrinks large numbers
log(1)      =  0
log(10)     =  2.3
log(100)    =  4.6
log(1000)   =  6.9
log(10000)  =  9.2

Numbers exploding from 1 to 10,000 — log compresses them to 0 to 9.2.

This is the core utility — when numbers span huge ranges, log brings them to a manageable scale.

Where they show up in ML — three places
1 — Loss function (log)

The standard loss function for classification is called cross-entropy loss:

Loss = -log(probability assigned to correct answer)

Real example:

Correct answer is "Paris"

Model A gives:  P("Paris") = 0.99  →  Loss = -log(0.99) = 0.01  ← tiny loss, good
Model B gives:  P("Paris") = 0.01  →  Loss = -log(0.01) = 4.60  ← huge loss, bad

The log naturally punishes wrong confident answers heavily and rewards correct confident answers.

2 — Probability multiplication becomes addition (log)

Say you want probability of a full sentence:

P("I love Java") = P("I") × P("love"|"I") × P("Java"|"I love")

= 0.3 × 0.2 × 0.15

= 0.009

Multiply enough small probabilities → number becomes microscopic → floating point underflow in your Java system. The number literally becomes 0.0 in memory.

Log fixes this:

log(A × B × C)  =  log(A) + log(B) + log(C)

Multiplication becomes addition. No underflow. This is why LLMs work in log probabilities internally.

3 — Exponential in softmax (e^x)

You already saw this. Raw logits → exponentiate → normalize → probabilities.

The relationship visually — in plain terms
e^x   →  small differences become huge differences  (amplify)
log   →  huge differences become small differences  (compress)

They're opposites — and ML uses both deliberately:

Softmax output    →  e^x amplifies the winner
Loss calculation  →  log compresses the range back to something trainable
